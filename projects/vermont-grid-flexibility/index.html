Vermont Grid Flexibility

How much can flexibility flatten net demand?

Summary

The power grid is one of the biggest machines people have ever built. It's a web of wires that ties together lots of places that make power: rivers turning turbines, wind spinning blades, sunlight landing on flat panels, and big plants burning fuel. Every moment, it pulls from the cheapest power it can get and sends it to your home. Each year, more of that power comes from sun, wind and water, which cost little to run and avoid pollution.

The grid has one hard rule: the power going into the wires has to match the power coming out, every second of every day.

So when we turn up our air conditioners, flip on the lights, add data centers and plug in our electric cars, a power plant has to turn up to meet them. But what if we could introduce flexibility into when we use power? How would this flexibility change the grid as we know it?

The nitty-gritty

This explorable shows the supply and demand of electricity during a hot summer week in Vermont. The demand includes normal electric use (base load), air conditioning (cooling) and 250,000 EVs (about half of all Vermont vehicles). You get to select your power supply, which has an impact on net demand (white region). In the section below, we explore flexibility with EV charging and how it can lower net demand while filling the deepest valleys where renewable energy is abundant.

What the heck is net demand?!

Net demand is the imbalance between supply and demand. When supply exceeds demand it drops below zero, indicating too much generation. When demand exceeds supply it rises above zero, indicating a capacity shortage. Net demand is calculated as total demand minus the total supply (solar, wind, hydro and gas). The white region plots this net demand, showing the gaps that need to be filled by new generation or flexible resources.

How the shifting works

The model doesn't target the highest peak directly. Instead it asks a yes-or-no question - can every hour of the week be held at or below some level? It then searches for the lowest level where the answer remains yes. That level becomes the flattest demand that flexibility can enable. In other words, it finds the flattest demand achievable with the available flexibility. It does so in these four steps:

  1. Split the charging. The flexible share of EV charging in a given hour can move; the rest stays put. Flexible charging may land anywhere within the shift window, but can never cause net demand to drop further below zero.
  2. Test a level. For a trial level, each hour has a certain amount of room before its net demand would rise above it. Walking the week in time order, the model fills each hour's room from the earliest charging that can still reach it. Earliest first matters: that charging is the first to run out of places to go. If all the movable charging finds a home, the level is reachable.
  3. Search for the lowest level. Try a level halfway between the best and worst cases. If it is reachable, try lower; if not, try higher. Repeating this hones in on the lowest reachable level, which is the new peak.
  4. Fill from the bottom. With the peak fixed, charging is placed by raising the level gradually from the deepest valley upward, so it pools where renewable energy is most abundant and the result is as flat as the windows allow.

Gas is dispatched afterwards against whatever shortfall remains, up to its capacity. In optimization terms this is a binary search on the peak with an earliest-deadline-first feasibility check, followed by water-filling. It gives an exact answer for this problem without a general-purpose solver.
